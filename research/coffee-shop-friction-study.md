# Coffee shops, routine, and friction

## An evidence-led study of how cafés become easy habits—and how the work behind the counter is made easier

**Research date:** 28 July 2026  
**Scope:** Coffee shops and bakery-cafés, with adjacent restaurant and retail evidence only where it answers an operating question that coffee-specific evidence does not.

## Executive conclusion

Coffee shops become part of a routine when they make the next visit both **easy to execute** and **easy to predict**. The strongest operators remove five customer costs:

1. **Access cost:** the shop is on an existing route, open at the needed time, or reachable through pickup/delivery.
2. **Decision cost:** the customer’s usual drink, modifiers, location, and payment method are remembered.
3. **Transaction cost:** ordering and payment take little time.
4. **waiting uncertainty:** the customer can trust when and where the order will be ready.
5. **risk cost:** taste, availability, recognition, and service are reliably familiar.

The important finding is that reducing front-end friction does not necessarily reduce total-system friction. Mobile ordering can make ordering effortless while creating an uncontrolled production queue. Starbucks’ 2024 experience is unusually clear: mobile orders were 31% of U.S. company-operated transactions, yet the company reported a **mid-teens mobile cart incompletion rate**, with customers citing long waits and unavailable products. The “frictionless front door” had exposed a constrained bar.

The most successful interventions therefore join the customer journey to the operating system:

- subscriptions and loyalty programs shape repeat demand;
- saved orders, stored payment, and nearby locations reduce repeat effort;
- visible, credible ready times reduce uncertainty;
- queue orchestration limits channel conflict;
- standard work and workstation redesign remove motion and task switching;
- demand-aligned staffing, stable schedules, and experienced managers reduce variability;
- inventory policies protect availability without excessive waste;
- a short feedback loop lets the people doing the work improve it.

This is not a case for maximum speed. It is a case for **low variance**. A reliably four-minute visit is easier to make habitual than a visit that sometimes takes two minutes and sometimes fifteen. Likewise, a warm interaction can reduce the felt cost of a wait, but hospitality cannot compensate indefinitely for an overloaded production system.

## 1. What “friction” means in a coffee shop

Service researchers Berry, Seiders, and Grewal separated convenience into decision, access, transaction, benefit, and post-benefit convenience. That is a better diagnostic than treating friction as “queue time” alone. In a café, friction appears before, during, and after the transaction.

| Stage | Customer friction | Corresponding operating friction |
|---|---|---|
| Choose | Unclear menu, too many modifiers, uncertain value | Excess SKUs, recipes, training burden |
| Reach | Location off-route, wrong hours, parking/drive-through difficulty | Poor demand forecast by location/daypart |
| Order | Queue, repeated customization, app/login/payment steps | Order entry, channel switching, unclear priority |
| Produce | Unknown wait, unavailable item, order error | Walking, reaching, batching, bottlenecks, stockouts |
| Handoff | Crowded counter, name confusion, missing food/drink | Mixed queues, search, remakes, incomplete orders |
| Return | No remembered preference, weak reason to repeat | No customer memory, inconsistent execution |
| Run the shop | — | Scheduling, training, inventory, maintenance, reconciliation, reporting |

This paired view matters. Every customer promise creates work. If the promise is not translated into capacity, sequencing, and standard work, friction is merely moved from the customer’s phone to the barista and eventually back to the customer as delay.

## 2. How coffee shops become part of an easy routine

### 2.1 Stable context is the foundation of habit

Habit research gives a precise reason coffee routines are powerful. In a 12-week real-world study, 96 people repeated an eating, drinking, or activity behavior in a stable context. Automaticity increased with repetition; among participants whose habit curve could be modeled, the time to reach 95% of their automaticity plateau ranged from **18 to 254 days**. Missing one opportunity did not materially derail formation. The mechanism is not simply frequency: it is frequency linked to a consistent cue such as “after breakfast,” “after school drop-off,” or “when I enter the office building.” [Lally et al., *European Journal of Social Psychology*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674)

A later randomized study supplied causal evidence: both stable-context and varied-context groups increased walking, but only the stable-context group developed and maintained habit automaticity. This was a walking study, not a coffee study, so it establishes the behavioral mechanism rather than a café sales effect. [Randomized consistent-context habit study, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39225981/)

**Observed implication in coffee:** successful convenience-led chains place the shop inside an existing cue. Luckin concentrated small pickup stores in office buildings, commercial areas, and university campuses. Its app recommended the nearest store, remembered preferred drink settings, put frequent purchases at the top, stored vouchers, and notified customers when orders were ready. In 2018, the company reported that **95.6% of orders were made through its app**; by March 2019 it had 16.8 million cumulative transacting customers. This is a tightly integrated routine system: route, choice, payment, production status, and pickup are all linked. [Luckin 2019 SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1767582/000104746919002450/a2238391zf-1.htm)

Luckin’s early filings must be treated cautiously because the company subsequently admitted fabricated sales and settled with the SEC. The product and operating design is well documented; early financial and customer metrics should not be treated as independently reliable proof of profitability.

### 2.2 A subscription turns “shall I?” into “where is the nearest one?”

The clearest measured evidence comes from Panera’s 100-day, 150-café coffee-subscription pilot. Panera’s CEO reported:

- customer visit frequency increased by **more than 200%**;
- food attachment increased **70%**;
- monthly renewal ran at **90–95%**.

The intervention reduced marginal price deliberation after the monthly fee had been paid. It also created a reason to pass through the café repeatedly, allowing food to attach to an already-decided visit. These are company-disclosed pilot results reported contemporaneously, but the public account does not provide store-level data, confidence intervals, or the full counterfactual design. [Panera pilot account in *Forbes*](https://www.forbes.com/sites/aliciakelso/2020/02/27/panera-launches-899-unlimited-monthly-coffee-subscription-program/) and [Restaurant Dive interview](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/paneras-new-coffee-subscription-leverages-loyalty-to-fight-breakfast-battl/573098/)

The effect endured at scale. By 2023 Panera said Sip Club members accounted for roughly **25% of transactions**; the company’s chief brand officer told the AP that members came several times a week and bought food about one-third of the time. [Associated Press report](https://apnews.com/article/7012f0ee968e761b257818658c515648)

Pret provides a second, larger-scale case:

- subscription redemptions rose from **34.7 million in 2021 to 57.9 million in 2022**—about 67%;
- Pret reported that sales per subscription transaction were **nearly 30% higher** than non-subscription transactions;
- digital reached **42% of transactions** in H1 2023;
- H1 2023 revenue rose **20.2%** year on year.

Pret attributed growth partly to Club Pret, but international openings and post-pandemic recovery also contributed. The 30% transaction difference is an association, not proof that the subscription caused all incremental spend. [Pret H1 2023 results](https://www.pret.com/en-GB/pr-pret-financial-2023)

Subscriptions have a capacity and trust boundary. Pret later replaced the up-to-five-drinks model with a discounted-drink offer. Panera has also revised price and terms. A program can create routine quickly, but changing a benefit customers have incorporated into daily life introduces unusually high perceived friction. The lesson from the observed cases is not “always subscribe”; it is that a subscription is a powerful demand-shaping instrument whose unit economics, peak-load effect, and promise durability must be designed together.

### 2.3 Progress and earned rewards accelerate the next visit

Kivetz, Urminsky, and Zheng studied an actual café reward program and ran related field experiments. Customers purchased coffee more frequently as they approached a free coffee. Giving a 12-stamp card with two “bonus” stamps—requiring the same ten purchases as a blank ten-stamp card—caused faster completion. Stronger acceleration also predicted retention and faster re-engagement after reward redemption. [Study in the *Journal of Marketing Research*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)

This supports visible, comprehensible progress, not complex points accounting. The mechanism reduces motivational distance; it does not reduce production time. It should therefore be evaluated separately from throughput.

### 2.4 Stored value and saved preferences compress repeat effort

Starbucks’ digital scale shows what happens when payment, loyalty, ordering, and customer memory are combined:

- in Q2 FY2024, mobile orders were **31% of U.S. company-operated transactions**;
- Starbucks Rewards members represented **59% of tender dollars**;
- the U.S. program had **32.8 million 90-day active members**;
- Starbucks Cards were **47% of transactions**.

[Starbucks Q2 FY2024 digital dashboard](https://investor.starbucks.com/files/doc_downloads/supplemental-financial/2024/q2-fy24-digital-ir-dashboard.pdf)

These figures do not prove that the app caused loyalty—loyal customers are also more likely to adopt the app—but they demonstrate that the habitual customer and the low-friction channel have become structurally linked. Starbucks itself says Rewards customers visit and spend more, while recognizing that its heaviest morning demand is routine-based.

### 2.5 Predictability matters as much as nominal speed

A 200-customer study in an international-chain coffee shop found that perceived waiting time reduced satisfaction. A favorable environment and employees moderated the harm. Ambience can make waiting feel less costly, but it does not add production capacity. [Coffee-shop waiting study](https://www.inderscience.com/info/inarticle.php?artid=48829)

The operating corollary is crucial: quote a ready time the shop can keep. Luckin supplied real-time status and routed delivery orders using distance, capacity, and inventory. Starbucks later invested in better wait estimates after customers abandoned mobile carts because of delay and availability. Uncertainty is friction even when the average wait is acceptable.

## 3. How operating friction has actually been reduced

### 3.1 Starbucks’ first lean transformation: observe work, remove motion, standardize flow

Starbucks’ 2008–2010 lean program is one of the best documented coffee-shop operating cases because it shows the method, store-level countermeasures, partner response, and business outcomes.

The system was not simply labor cutting. Teams observed work at the “gemba,” broke beverage production into repeatable routines, used visual signals, replenished to demand, and gave stores a method for local problem solving.

One documented beverage trial compared the same sequence of six drinks:

- old routine: **4 minutes 56 seconds**;
- new routine: **3 minutes 52 seconds**;
- improvement: **64 seconds, or 21.6%**.

At another store, the team converted a cold-water dispenser to a pitcher rinser and placed ice at the espresso bar. Starbucks reported **more than 90% less unnecessary motion**, less walking and stress, faster service, and a change from negative to positive transaction comparisons while comparable stores remained negative. [Starbucks/Lean Enterprise Institute presentation](https://www.lean.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/starbucks_plenary.pdf)

At company level, Starbucks reported that fiscal 2009 customer satisfaction increased **10 percentage points**. On the Q3 2009 call it reported improvements since January in friendliness (+7 points), beverage taste (+7), speed (+11), and overall satisfaction (+9). Cost reductions, store closures, retraining, marketing, and other changes occurred simultaneously, so the company-wide gains cannot be attributed to lean alone. [Starbucks FY2009 annual report](https://media.corporate-ir.net/media_files/irol/99/99518/SBUX_AR.pdf) and [Q3 2009 earnings-call transcript](https://www.roic.ai/quote/SBUX%3AAV/transcripts/2009-year/3-quarter)

The systematic features were:

1. direct observation rather than manager opinion;
2. a baseline time and motion record;
3. a small physical or sequencing countermeasure;
4. remeasurement;
5. standardization of what worked;
6. store-level permission to continue improving it.

That is a stronger explanation of the result than “work faster.” The intervention reduced non-value work so the same effort produced more finished drinks.

### 3.2 Starbucks’ second operating lesson: digital demand must be gated and sequenced

By 2024 Starbucks had made ordering highly convenient but production demand could arrive without a physical queue. Its Q2 earnings call disclosed:

- mobile order and pay reached **31% of transactions**;
- mobile cart incompletion was in the **mid-teens**, with wait time and product availability cited;
- tests of its no-capital Siren Craft operating method increased peak throughput enough to be worth an estimated **nearly one comparable-sales point annually**;
- store operating efficiencies improved **150 basis points** in the quarter.

[Starbucks Q2 FY2024 transcript](https://investor.starbucks.com/files/doc_events/2024/04/SBUX-Corrected-Transcript.pdf)

The later “Back to Starbucks” turnaround explicitly treated this as a whole-system problem. Starbucks simplified the menu, added peak labor, introduced Smart Queue sequencing across café/mobile/drive-through channels, restored service standards, and increased local ownership. By Q4 FY2025, **80% of U.S. company-operated cafés were averaging café handoff in four minutes or less**, including on a record fall launch day; global comparable sales returned to growth after seven quarters. By Q1 FY2026, U.S. transactions grew **3%**, the first increase in eight quarters. These were bundled interventions, so no public evidence isolates the contribution of menu reduction, staffing, queue software, or hospitality. [Starbucks FY2025 results](https://investor.starbucks.com/news/financial-releases/news-details/2025/Starbucks-Reports-Q4-and-Full-Fiscal-Year-2025-Results/default.aspx) and [2026 turnaround report](https://www.axios.com/2026/01/28/starbucks-earnings-traffic-growth-turnaround)

This turnaround also makes a less comfortable point: operating ease required spending. Starbucks said it invested more than **$500 million** in staffing, scheduling, and support. By April 2026 it reported:

- 98% of available shifts filled;
- nearly 95% of partners receiving preferred schedules;
- record retention and turnover nearly half the industry average;
- about 80% of U.S. company-operated cafés delivering café orders in four minutes or less;
- the share of stores meeting its highest performance standard up more than 30%.

These are company claims, not independently audited operating measures, but their direction is consistent with broader service and scheduling research. [Starbucks partner investment report](https://about.starbucks.com/press/2026/starbucks-investments-in-coffeehouse-partners-are-paying-off/)

### 3.3 Inventory: replace intuition with SKU-specific service levels

A 2024 peer-reviewed case study of a compact Thai coffee shop tested three periodic-review policies for perishables. The best policies used daily rather than weekly demand, smaller orders, more frequent deliveries, and SKU service levels based on the item’s profit/cost ratio. The modeled annual inventory cost fell **60.74%, or about THB 256,922**, while freshness improved. This was a computational case based on real shop data, not a live randomized implementation, so it is strong evidence of a feasible policy but not an observed savings claim. [Engineering Journal case study](https://engj.org/index.php/ej/article/view/4557)

A supplier case study reports an implemented example at Road Café, a 20-plus-store Saudi drive-in chain. Integrating outlet POS, inventory consumption, finance, and intercompany transactions reportedly reduced **waste 15%** and **manual effort 40%**. Because LS Retail produced the case, the figures should be treated as customer/vendor-reported rather than independent evaluation. [LS Retail/Road Café case summary](https://www.casestudies.com/company/ls-retail/case-study/road-cafe-customer-case-study)

Together the cases support three operating practices:

- forecast and set service levels by SKU rather than applying one blanket par;
- shorten the review/delivery cycle for volatile perishables;
- connect the sale, recipe depletion, physical count, waste, and reorder so staff do not reconcile separate systems.

### 3.4 Labor: align to demand without creating employee instability

Pressed Café, an 11-location high-volume café/restaurant group, used daypart-level labor visibility to identify overstaffing before lunch, after lunch, and at dinner. At one location, the vendor reports that targeted changes reduced weekly labor spending by **more than 5%** without blind across-the-board cuts; the group estimated about **$500,000 annual savings** and seven-figure potential at scale. It is useful practitioner evidence, but it is a software supplier’s case study and does not publish service, retention, or store-level financial data. [Pressed Café case study](https://www.fourth.com/case-study/pressed-cafe-case-study)

The strongest causal scheduling evidence is outside coffee but highly relevant to hourly service work. In a randomized trial at 28 Gap stores, a bundle of more stable scheduling practices increased sales **3.3%** and labor productivity **5.1%** while labor hours fell **1.8%**. The intervention stores generated an estimated **$2.9 million** in incremental revenue on **$31,200** of centrally funded additional staffing. Because the bundle included targeted extra hours in some stores, the result cannot be interpreted as schedule consistency alone. [Stable Scheduling Study report](https://www.worklifelaw.org/publications/Stable-Scheduling-Study-Report.pdf)

Coffee-specific research explains why this can reach the customer. A study matching **263 coffee-shop employees with 973 customers** in South Korea found that job satisfaction correlated with customer-perceived value, which in turn correlated with customer satisfaction; a positive service climate improved the chain. It is correlational, not causal, but it directly rejects the idea that employee friction is invisible to customers. [Coffee-shop service-profit-chain study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0278431921000098)

The evidence points toward demand-aligned but stable staffing: use short demand intervals to place labor, yet give people adequate hours, advance notice, fair distribution, shift-swapping tools, and experienced floor leadership. A labor model that optimizes only paid minutes can raise training, absence, turnover, and service variability—the costs that the model does not see.

## 4. What did not work, or worked only partially

### Friction was moved, not removed

Mobile order-ahead removed the visible register queue but allowed demand to flood the bar. In operations terms, customer work-in-progress became invisible and effectively unbounded. Research on omnichannel service queues confirms that the throughput-maximizing priority depends on capacity and customer patience; the wrong channel-priority rule can materially reduce throughput and welfare. [Omnichannel queue research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10591478241231874)

**Observed correction:** integrate channels into one production queue, quote capacity-aware times, and gate or slow acceptance when capacity is exhausted.

### More choice created both customer value and operating complexity

Customization supports identity and loyalty, but each modifier adds decision time, recipe paths, stock positions, training, and error opportunities. Starbucks’ 2025 plan to remove roughly 30% of menu items was an explicit response to this complexity. The subsequent turnaround improved service and traffic, but because staffing and queue changes occurred together, the public evidence does not establish menu reduction as the sole cause.

**Observed correction:** protect high-frequency/customization value while removing low-volume items and ingredients that add disproportionate work.

### Cost cutting was confused with lean

The Starbucks lean material emphasizes reducing walking, searching, batching, and uneven work. Later barista and union accounts describe understaffing and uncontrollable mobile queues. These are different operating philosophies. Removing waste creates capacity; removing capacity while demand and complexity remain creates queues and burnout.

**Observed correction:** measure customer handoff, quality, remakes, availability, employee strain, and labor cost together. A labor saving that worsens the first four is not a system saving.

### Subscription success created peak demand and promise risk

Panera and Pret demonstrated large frequency and attachment effects. Both also changed their programs over time. When a subscription becomes a routine, benefit reduction feels like a disruption to a daily contract, not an ordinary price change.

**Observed correction:** test load by 15-minute interval, food attachment, heavy-user economics, renewal, and cancellation before scaling; reserve the right operating capacity before marketing the promise.

### Technology added a new failure mode

App login, incorrect store selection, inaccurate ready times, payment problems, and a crowded pickup shelf can each replace a familiar queue with a less legible one. Luckin’s app-only model also excludes customers who cannot or will not use the required digital channel.

**Observed correction:** maintain a recovery path, show the chosen store and ready time prominently, and design handoff as carefully as checkout.

## 5. The recurring method behind successful reductions

Across the strongest cases, friction was reduced systematically in the following sequence.

### 1. Define the job in customer terms

The job is not “make coffee.” It might be “let me collect the same drink between the school run and work without being late” or “give me a dependable place and welcome between meetings.” This determines whether access, speed, recognition, seating, or predictability is the dominant friction.

### 2. Map one order end to end

Include customer decisions, digital steps, tickets, walking, reaching, waiting, batching, replenishment, handoff, remakes, and recovery. Starbucks’ 90% motion reduction came from observing the physical path, not from a high-level dashboard.

### 3. Baseline flow and variance, not only averages

Measure by 15-minute interval and channel:

- arrivals, accepted orders, completed orders;
- promised and actual ready time;
- median and 90th-percentile wait;
- cart/order abandonment;
- stockouts and substitutions;
- remakes;
- touches, steps, and context switches per order;
- labor hours, overtime, absence, and schedule changes;
- waste and variance between theoretical and actual usage.

The 90th percentile matters because routines are broken by the bad day, not the average day.

### 4. Remove physical and informational waste first

Co-locate high-frequency items; give each role a replenishment signal; separate production from searching; connect POS, recipe depletion, waste, and ordering; make queue state visible. These changes create capacity without asking people to move faster.

### 5. Standardize the best current method

Starbucks’ repeatable beverage routine and Playbook created a stable baseline that could absorb peaks and expose problems. Standard work is the current best sequence, not a ban on judgment or improvement.

### 6. Control release into the bottleneck

Order acceptance and promise times must reflect the constrained station—often espresso, cold bar, oven, or handoff. A digital channel should not release work faster than the system can finish it.

### 7. Match demand and staffing while protecting schedule quality

Forecast by daypart, product mix, and channel. Place experienced people at constraint periods. Use stable core schedules and flexible voluntary coverage rather than last-minute instability. Starbucks’ recent results and the Gap experiment both suggest that labor stability can be productive capacity.

### 8. Pilot, compare, and remeasure

Panera used 150 cafés over roughly 100 days. Starbucks tested specific bar routines and later its Siren Craft system. Compare pilot stores or shifts with similar controls, pre-register the measures, and include unintended effects such as employee workload and non-digital customer wait.

### 9. Let frontline teams continue the loop

The early Starbucks method spread because stores learned to identify and solve work problems. Central technology can expose a problem, but the person standing at the bar often sees the unnecessary motion, missing signal, or conflicting rule first.

## 6. Evidence-backed operating design by friction type

These are not speculative feature ideas; each is the operating pattern demonstrated in the cases above.

| Friction to reduce | Demonstrated mechanism | Best observed evidence | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeated decision effort | Saved favorites, simple reorder, visible reward progress | Luckin design; café goal-gradient field study | Do not hide price or make rewards hard to value |
| Price deliberation on each visit | Beverage subscription | Panera +200% frequency pilot; Pret 57.9m redemptions | Model peak capacity and durable economics |
| Access | Dense pickup points on existing routes | Luckin office/campus pickup network | Convenience format must fit local regulation and inclusion |
| Register queue | Stored payment and order-ahead | Starbucks 31% mobile share; Luckin 95.6% app orders | Gate demand at production capacity |
| Wait uncertainty | Capacity-aware promise and order status | Luckin status/routing; Starbucks wait-estimate work | Accuracy is more important than optimistic times |
| Barista motion | Planted stations, item co-location, rinser/ice at bar | Starbucks >90% motion reduction example | Preserve ergonomics, quality, and interaction |
| Production variation | Repeatable beverage routine and visual work | Starbucks six-drink sequence 21.6% faster | Standardize with baristas, not to them |
| Channel collision | Unified, intelligent sequencing | Starbucks Smart Queue/Green Apron bundled turnaround | Track service fairness by channel |
| Stockouts and waste | SKU-specific service levels; shorter review cycles | Thai case modeled 60.74% cost reduction | Validate delivery cost and live results |
| Manual reconciliation | Integrated POS, inventory, finance | Road Café reported 15% less waste, 40% less manual effort | Vendor-reported; verify locally |
| Misallocated labor | Daypart visibility and targeted adjustment | Pressed Café vendor-reported >5% labor reduction | Track service and employee outcomes |
| Turnover and absence | Preferred, stable schedules; adequate peak staffing | Starbucks company results; Gap randomized experiment | Avoid using flexibility as employee risk transfer |

## 7. What the evidence does and does not establish

### High confidence

- Repetition in a stable context creates automaticity.
- Visible progress toward a café reward accelerates purchases.
- Uncertain or perceived long waits reduce coffee-shop satisfaction.
- Physical observation, workstation redesign, and repeatable sequencing can materially reduce motion and production time.
- A digital ordering channel can overload production if release is not capacity-controlled.
- More stable scheduling can improve sales and labor productivity in hourly retail.

### Moderate confidence

- Coffee subscriptions can sharply increase frequency and food attachment. The effects are large and repeated across Panera and Pret, but public data is company-reported.
- Integrated inventory and labor systems reduce administrative effort and waste. Results are plausible and consistent, but much of the implementation evidence is vendor-produced.
- Employee stability improves the customer experience. Coffee-specific matched data supports the chain, but causal coffee-shop trials are limited.

### Not established by the public evidence

- That an app by itself creates loyalty rather than attracting already-loyal users.
- That maximum customization is more profitable after its full operating cost.
- That automation or self-service always improves total throughput.
- That the fastest store creates the strongest routine; predictability and relational quality may matter more.
- That a subscription remains profitable at every location and peak mix.
- That labor-cost reduction is beneficial when turnover, quality, remakes, or lost demand are included.

## Final synthesis

The easy coffee routine is a repeated exchange of certainty:

> same cue → easy access → familiar choice → low-effort payment → credible wait → reliable product → recognizable welcome

The corresponding operating system is:

> visible demand → controlled release → clear priority → standard work → supplied station → capable, stable team → measured result → local improvement

The two loops must be designed together. Customer friction falls sustainably only when work friction falls with it. The cases with the clearest gains—Starbucks’ lean routines, Panera’s subscription pilot, Pret’s scaled membership, Luckin’s integrated pickup model, and Starbucks’ later queue-and-staffing turnaround—each joined multiple steps into a system. The clearest failures occurred when one step was optimized in isolation.

That is the central finding: a coffee shop becomes an easy routine not by eliminating every moment of effort, but by making the important moments **consistent, legible, and dependable** for customers and staff alike.
